Density functional theory (DFT) is the method behind most electronic-structure calculations on molecules and solids today, from a geometry optimisation in Gaussian to a band structure in Quantum ESPRESSO. This page lays out the chain of ideas in order, with each equation written out, so that the input keywords of either program have a clear meaning. A longer version, chapter by chapter with runnable Python, is in my DFT notes.

#The problem DFT solves

A molecule is a set of nuclei and electrons. A proton is about 1836 times heavier than an electron, so the electrons adjust almost instantly to any motion of the nuclei. The Born–Oppenheimer approximation [1] uses this to separate the two: fix the nuclei at positions RA\mathbf R_ARA​, solve for the electrons, and treat the resulting electronic energy as the potential energy surface on which the nuclei move. In atomic units the electronic Hamiltonian for NNN electrons is

H^=−12∑i=1N∇i2+∑i=1Nvext(ri)+∑i<j1∣ri−rj∣,vext(r)=−∑AZA∣r−RA∣.\hat H = -\frac12 \sum_{i=1}^{N} \nabla_i^2 + \sum_{i=1}^{N} v_\mathrm{ext}(\mathbf r_i) + \sum_{i<j} \frac{1}{|\mathbf r_i - \mathbf r_j|}, \qquad v_\mathrm{ext}(\mathbf r) = -\sum_A \frac{Z_A}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf R_A|} .H^=−21​i=1∑N​∇i2​+i=1∑N​vext​(ri​)+i<j∑​∣ri​−rj​∣1​,vext​(r)=−A∑​∣r−RA​∣ZA​​.

The three terms are the kinetic energy, the attraction to the nuclei, and the electron–electron repulsion. The last term couples every electron to every other, so the wavefunction Ψ(x1,…,xN)\Psi(\mathbf x_1, \dots, \mathbf x_N)Ψ(x1​,…,xN​) cannot be split into one-electron pieces and depends on all 3N3N3N spatial coordinates at once (plus spins). Storing it on even a coarse grid of 10 points per coordinate would take 103N10^{3N}103N numbers, which for the 10 electrons of a water molecule is already 103010^{30}1030.

The electron density

n(r)=N∑s1∫∣Ψ(rs1,x2,…,xN)∣2 dx2⋯dxNn(\mathbf r) = N \sum_{s_1} \int |\Psi(\mathbf r s_1, \mathbf x_2, \dots, \mathbf x_N)|^2 \, d\mathbf x_2 \cdots d\mathbf x_Nn(r)=Ns1​∑​∫∣Ψ(rs1​,x2​,…,xN​)∣2dx2​⋯dxN​

is a function of only three variables, whatever NNN is. DFT is the statement that this much smaller object is enough.

#The Hohenberg–Kohn theorems (1964)

Hohenberg and Kohn proved two results [2].

First theorem. For a non-degenerate ground state, the density n0(r)n_0(\mathbf r)n0​(r) determines the external potential vext(r)v_\mathrm{ext}(\mathbf r)vext​(r) up to an additive constant. The proof is short. Suppose two potentials that differ by more than a constant had the same ground-state density n0n_0n0​, with ground states Ψ1\Psi_1Ψ1​, Ψ2\Psi_2Ψ2​ and energies E1E_1E1​, E2E_2E2​. Using Ψ2\Psi_2Ψ2​ as a trial function for H^1\hat H_1H^1​ gives E1<E2+∫(v1−v2) n0 drE_1 < E_2 + \int (v_1 - v_2)\,n_0\,d\mathbf rE1​<E2​+∫(v1​−v2​)n0​dr, and the same argument the other way round gives E2<E1+∫(v2−v1) n0 drE_2 < E_1 + \int (v_2 - v_1)\,n_0\,d\mathbf rE2​<E1​+∫(v2​−v1​)n0​dr. Adding the two gives E1+E2<E1+E2E_1 + E_2 < E_1 + E_2E1​+E2​<E1​+E2​, a contradiction. Since vextv_\mathrm{ext}vext​ and N=∫n drN = \int n\,d\mathbf rN=∫ndr fix the Hamiltonian, the ground-state density fixes everything about the system, including its energy.

Second theorem. There is a universal functional F[n]=⟨Ψ∣T^+V^ee∣Ψ⟩F[n] = \langle \Psi | \hat T + \hat V_{ee} | \Psi \rangleF[n]=⟨Ψ∣T^+V^ee​∣Ψ⟩, the same for every molecule, such that

Ev[n]=F[n]+∫vext(r) n(r) dr ≥ E0E_v[n] = F[n] + \int v_\mathrm{ext}(\mathbf r)\, n(\mathbf r)\, d\mathbf r \ \ge\ E_0Ev​[n]=F[n]+∫vext​(r)n(r)dr ≥ E0​

for any admissible density, with equality at the true ground-state density. The ground state can therefore be found by minimising over densities instead of wavefunctions. Levy's constrained search [3] later gave F[n]F[n]F[n] a definition that works for any density coming from an NNN-electron wavefunction, including degenerate ground states.

The theorems prove that F[n]F[n]F[n] exists. They do not say what it is, and the kinetic-energy part in particular is very hard to write directly in terms of nnn.

#The Kohn–Sham construction (1965)

Kohn and Sham's idea [4] was to borrow the kinetic energy from a system that is easy to solve. Imagine NNN non-interacting electrons moving in some effective potential vs(r)v_s(\mathbf r)vs​(r), chosen so that their ground-state density equals the density of the real, interacting system. Their wavefunction is a single Slater determinant of orbitals ϕi\phi_iϕi​, and

n(r)=∑i=1N∣ϕi(r)∣2n(\mathbf r) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} |\phi_i(\mathbf r)|^2n(r)=i=1∑N​∣ϕi​(r)∣2

(summing over occupied spin orbitals). The total energy is split into four pieces, three of which are known exactly:

E[n]=Ts[n]+Eext[n]+EH[n]+Exc[n],E[n] = T_s[n] + E_\mathrm{ext}[n] + E_\mathrm{H}[n] + E_\mathrm{xc}[n],E[n]=Ts​[n]+Eext​[n]+EH​[n]+Exc​[n],
Ts=−12∑i=1N⟨ϕi∣∇2∣ϕi⟩,Eext=∫vext(r) n(r) dr,EH=12∬n(r) n(r′)∣r−r′∣ dr dr′.T_s = -\frac12 \sum_{i=1}^{N} \langle \phi_i | \nabla^2 | \phi_i \rangle, \qquad E_\mathrm{ext} = \int v_\mathrm{ext}(\mathbf r)\, n(\mathbf r)\, d\mathbf r, \qquad E_\mathrm{H} = \frac12 \iint \frac{n(\mathbf r)\, n(\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r'|}\, d\mathbf r\, d\mathbf r' .Ts​=−21​i=1∑N​⟨ϕi​∣∇2∣ϕi​⟩,Eext​=∫vext​(r)n(r)dr,EH​=21​∬∣r−r′∣n(r)n(r′)​drdr′.

TsT_sTs​ is the kinetic energy of the non-interacting electrons, EextE_\mathrm{ext}Eext​ the attraction to the nuclei, and EHE_\mathrm{H}EH​ the Hartree energy, the classical repulsion of the density with itself. Everything else goes into the exchange–correlation energy, defined as the remainder:

Exc[n]=(T[n]−Ts[n])+(Vee[n]−EH[n]).E_\mathrm{xc}[n] = \left(T[n] - T_s[n]\right) + \left(V_{ee}[n] - E_\mathrm{H}[n]\right).Exc​[n]=(T[n]−Ts​[n])+(Vee​[n]−EH​[n]).

It contains exchange, correlation, the part of the true kinetic energy that TsT_sTs​ misses, and the correction for the self-repulsion that EHE_\mathrm{H}EH​ wrongly includes. It is a modest fraction of the total energy, but chemical bonding depends on it sensitively, and it is where all the approximation in practical DFT lives.

Minimising E[n]E[n]E[n] with respect to the orbitals, keeping them orthonormal, gives the Kohn–Sham equations:

[−12∇2+vext(r)+vH(r)+vxc(r)]ϕi(r)=εi ϕi(r),\left[ -\frac12 \nabla^2 + v_\mathrm{ext}(\mathbf r) + v_\mathrm{H}(\mathbf r) + v_\mathrm{xc}(\mathbf r) \right] \phi_i(\mathbf r) = \varepsilon_i\, \phi_i(\mathbf r),[−21​∇2+vext​(r)+vH​(r)+vxc​(r)]ϕi​(r)=εi​ϕi​(r),
vH(r)=∫n(r′)∣r−r′∣ dr′,vxc(r)=δExc[n]δn(r).v_\mathrm{H}(\mathbf r) = \int \frac{n(\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r'|}\, d\mathbf r', \qquad v_\mathrm{xc}(\mathbf r) = \frac{\delta E_\mathrm{xc}[n]}{\delta n(\mathbf r)} .vH​(r)=∫∣r−r′∣n(r′)​dr′,vxc​(r)=δn(r)δExc​[n]​.

These look like one-electron Schrödinger equations, and with the exact ExcE_\mathrm{xc}Exc​ they would give the exact ground-state density and energy. The total energy is not simply the sum of the eigenvalues, because ∑iεi\sum_i \varepsilon_i∑i​εi​ counts the Hartree energy twice and includes ∫vxc n\int v_\mathrm{xc}\, n∫vxc​n instead of ExcE_\mathrm{xc}Exc​:

E=∑i=1Nεi−EH[n]−∫vxc(r) n(r) dr+Exc[n]+ENN,E = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \varepsilon_i - E_\mathrm{H}[n] - \int v_\mathrm{xc}(\mathbf r)\, n(\mathbf r)\, d\mathbf r + E_\mathrm{xc}[n] + E_{NN},E=i=1∑N​εi​−EH​[n]−∫vxc​(r)n(r)dr+Exc​[n]+ENN​,

where ENNE_{NN}ENN​ is the repulsion between the fixed nuclei.

For comparison, Hückel theory also solves a one-electron eigenvalue problem, but its Hamiltonian is fixed in advance by two parameters. In Kohn–Sham theory the Hamiltonian depends on the density it produces, which is why the equations have to be solved iteratively.

#The self-consistent field loop

  1. Guess a density, for example a superposition of atomic densities.
  2. Build the effective potential vext+vH[n]+vxc[n]v_\mathrm{ext} + v_\mathrm{H}[n] + v_\mathrm{xc}[n]vext​+vH​[n]+vxc​[n].
  3. Solve the Kohn–Sham equations for the lowest orbitals. In a basis set this becomes a matrix eigenvalue problem.
  4. Build a new density from the occupied orbitals.
  5. Mix the new density with the old one rather than replacing it outright, which damps oscillations. Simple linear mixing works for easy cases; codes use extrapolation schemes such as Pulay's DIIS (common in molecular codes, applied to the Kohn–Sham matrix) or Broyden-type density mixing (used in Quantum ESPRESSO).
  6. Repeat from step 2 until the energy and density stop changing within a set threshold.

A geometry optimisation wraps a second loop around this one: converge the SCF, compute forces on the nuclei, move them, and converge again.

#Jacob's ladder of functionals

Perdew and Schmidt arranged approximations to ExcE_\mathrm{xc}Exc​ as the rungs of a ladder [5]. Each rung adds one more ingredient, and with it more physics and more cost.

Rung Ingredients Examples
Local density approximation (LDA) n(r)n(\mathbf r)n(r) Slater exchange with VWN correlation
Generalised gradient approximation (GGA) nnn, ∇n\nabla n∇n PBE, BLYP
Meta-GGA nnn, ∇n\nabla n∇n, kinetic energy density τ\tauτ TPSS, SCAN
Hybrid a GGA or meta-GGA plus a fraction of exact exchange from occupied orbitals B3LYP, PBE0
Fifth rung unoccupied orbitals as well double hybrids, random-phase approximation

#LDA

The LDA treats each point of the molecule as if it were a uniform electron gas at the local density:

ExcLDA=∫n(r) εxcunif ⁣(n(r))dr.E_\mathrm{xc}^\mathrm{LDA} = \int n(\mathbf r)\, \varepsilon_\mathrm{xc}^\mathrm{unif}\!\left(n(\mathbf r)\right) d\mathbf r .ExcLDA​=∫n(r)εxcunif​(n(r))dr.

The exchange part is known exactly, ExLDA=−34(3π)1/3∫n4/3 drE_\mathrm{x}^\mathrm{LDA} = -\tfrac34 \left(\tfrac{3}{\pi}\right)^{1/3} \int n^{4/3}\, d\mathbf rExLDA​=−43​(π3​)1/3∫n4/3dr, where the constant is 0.7386 for a spin-unpolarised density. The correlation part comes from quantum Monte Carlo calculations on the uniform gas [6], fitted to analytic forms such as that of Vosko, Wilk and Nusair [7]. LDA works surprisingly well for solids with slowly varying densities but overbinds molecules.

#GGA, for example PBE

A GGA also uses the gradient of the density. Exchange is written as the LDA value times an enhancement factor that depends on the reduced gradient s=∣∇n∣/(2kFn)s = |\nabla n| / (2 k_F n)s=∣∇n∣/(2kF​n), with kF=(3π2n)1/3k_F = (3\pi^2 n)^{1/3}kF​=(3π2n)1/3. In PBE [8]

Fx(s)=1+κ−κ1+μs2/κ,κ=0.804,μ=0.21951.F_\mathrm{x}(s) = 1 + \kappa - \frac{\kappa}{1 + \mu s^2/\kappa}, \qquad \kappa = 0.804, \quad \mu = 0.21951 .Fx​(s)=1+κ−1+μs2/κκ​,κ=0.804,μ=0.21951.

Neither constant is fitted to molecular data. κ\kappaκ is set by the Lieb–Oxford bound on the exchange energy, which caps FxF_\mathrm{x}Fx​ at 1+κ=1.8041 + \kappa = 1.8041+κ=1.804, and μ\muμ is chosen so that for slowly varying densities the gradient terms of exchange and correlation cancel, keeping the LDA's correct linear response. PBE is the usual default for solids in plane-wave codes.

#Hybrids, for example B3LYP

Hartree–Fock exchange, evaluated with the Kohn–Sham orbitals, is exact for exchange but pairs badly with approximate correlation. Becke showed that mixing a fraction of it into a GGA improves thermochemistry, and proposed a three-parameter form fitted to the atomisation energies, ionisation potentials, proton affinities and total atomic energies of a standard test set [9]:

Exc=ExcLSDA+a0(Exexact−ExLSDA)+ax ΔExB88+ac ΔEcPW91,E_\mathrm{xc} = E_\mathrm{xc}^\mathrm{LSDA} + a_0 \left( E_\mathrm{x}^\mathrm{exact} - E_\mathrm{x}^\mathrm{LSDA} \right) + a_\mathrm{x}\, \Delta E_\mathrm{x}^\mathrm{B88} + a_\mathrm{c}\, \Delta E_\mathrm{c}^\mathrm{PW91},Exc​=ExcLSDA​+a0​(Exexact​−ExLSDA​)+ax​ΔExB88​+ac​ΔEcPW91​,

with a0=0.20a_0 = 0.20a0​=0.20, ax=0.72a_\mathrm{x} = 0.72ax​=0.72 and ac=0.81a_\mathrm{c} = 0.81ac​=0.81. Here ΔExB88\Delta E_\mathrm{x}^\mathrm{B88}ΔExB88​ is Becke's 1988 gradient correction to exchange [10]. B3LYP keeps the same three numbers but replaces the PW91 correlation with that of Lee, Yang and Parr (LYP) [11, 12]. LYP is a complete correlation functional rather than a correction to the local one, so the local part is mixed in separately. As Gaussian implements it [13],

ExcB3LYP=0.80 ExSlater+0.20 ExHF+0.72 ΔExB88+0.81 EcLYP+0.19 EcVWN.E_\mathrm{xc}^\mathrm{B3LYP} = 0.80\, E_\mathrm{x}^\mathrm{Slater} + 0.20\, E_\mathrm{x}^\mathrm{HF} + 0.72\, \Delta E_\mathrm{x}^\mathrm{B88} + 0.81\, E_\mathrm{c}^\mathrm{LYP} + 0.19\, E_\mathrm{c}^\mathrm{VWN} .ExcB3LYP​=0.80ExSlater​+0.20ExHF​+0.72ΔExB88​+0.81EcLYP​+0.19EcVWN​.

Gaussian uses Vosko–Wilk–Nusair functional III for the local correlation, while some other programs use functional V, so B3LYP energies from different codes can differ slightly. The exact-exchange term depends on the orbitals, not just the density, so it is a non-local operator and costs more than a GGA.

#Basis sets and plane waves

The Kohn–Sham orbitals have to be expanded in some set of functions, and the two programs I use make different choices.

Gaussian: atom-centred Gaussian basis sets. Each orbital is a combination of Gaussian functions xaybzce−αr2x^a y^b z^c e^{-\alpha r^2}xaybzce−αr2 centred on the atoms. The functions follow the molecule, so a small number of them describes a molecule well, all electrons can be treated explicitly for light atoms (with effective core potentials available for heavy ones), and the integrals needed for exact exchange have closed forms, which makes hybrid functionals routine. The quality is improved by moving to larger basis sets, and because each atom's functions also help describe its neighbours, interaction energies carry a basis-set superposition error that has to be checked.

Quantum ESPRESSO: plane waves with pseudopotentials. For a periodic crystal, Bloch's theorem writes each orbital as eik⋅re^{i\mathbf k \cdot \mathbf r}eik⋅r times a function with the period of the lattice, and that function is expanded in plane waves eiG⋅re^{i\mathbf G \cdot \mathbf r}eiG⋅r over the reciprocal lattice vectors G\mathbf GG. All plane waves with 12∣k+G∣2≤Ecut\tfrac12 |\mathbf k + \mathbf G|^2 \le E_\mathrm{cut}21​∣k+G∣2≤Ecut​ are kept, so a single number controls the quality of the basis and there is no superposition error [14]. The price is that the sharp orbitals near a nucleus would need an enormous cutoff. Pseudopotentials (norm-conserving or ultrasoft) and the closely related projector augmented-wave (PAW) method remove the core electrons and smooth the valence orbitals inside a core radius, which brings the cutoff down to something practical. The Brillouin zone has to be sampled with a grid of k\mathbf kk points, molecules have to be placed in a box with enough vacuum to separate them from their periodic images, and exact exchange is much more expensive than with Gaussian functions, which is one reason GGAs such as PBE dominate plane-wave work.

#Where this meets my own work

I used B3LYP in Gaussian for a DFT study of the vanadium complex [VO(SALIEP)(DTP)] as a water-reducing catalyst, with Dr. Soumyajit Roy. The work is in preparation, so there are no results to report here. A single molecular complex is the case that atom-centred basis sets and hybrid functionals suit well, while periodic solids belong to the plane-wave side. Longer derivations of these steps, including the Hartree–Fock background that the hybrids borrow from, are in the DFT notes.
