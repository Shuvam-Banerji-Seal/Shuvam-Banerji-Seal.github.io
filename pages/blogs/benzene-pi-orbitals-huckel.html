Drawing benzene's π orbitals from Hückel theory

How the benzene figure on the homepage is made: Hückel π orbitals, Slater 2p functions, and points sampled from the probability density, coloured by sign.

The animated figure on the homepage of this site shows the six π molecular orbitals (MOs) of benzene as clouds of coloured points. The orbital shapes come from Hückel theory and a Slater-type atomic orbital, and the points come from a random sampler. This note goes through each step, with every number checked in Python, so that anyone looking at the figure knows exactly what it shows and what it leaves out.

#The molecule

The figure uses an idealised, planar benzene. The six carbons sit on a regular hexagon in the xyxyxy plane at angles θk=2πk/6\theta_k = 2\pi k/6θk​=2πk/6 for k=0,1,…,5k = 0, 1, \dots, 5k=0,1,…,5. In a regular hexagon the side equals the distance from the centre to a corner, so a C–C bond length of 1.39 Å puts every carbon 1.39 Å from the centre:

Rk=R (cos⁡θk, sin⁡θk, 0),R=1.39 A˚.\mathbf{R}_k = R\,(\cos\theta_k,\ \sin\theta_k,\ 0), \qquad R = 1.39\ \text{Å}.Rk​=R(cosθk​, sinθk​, 0),R=1.39 A˚.

Each hydrogen lies on the same ray as its carbon, 1.09 Å further out, so 2.48 Å from the centre. The hydrogens are drawn for orientation only and take no part in the π system.

#The Hückel model

Hückel's model treats the π electrons of a planar conjugated molecule separately from the σ framework [1, 4]. Each carbon contributes one 2pz2p_z2pz​ orbital ϕk\phi_kϕk​, and a π MO is a linear combination

ψ=∑k=05ck ϕk.\psi = \sum_{k=0}^{5} c_k\,\phi_k .ψ=k=0∑5​ck​ϕk​.

The variation principle turns the Schrödinger equation into the secular equations ∑l(Hkl−E Skl) cl=0\sum_l (H_{kl} - E\,S_{kl})\,c_l = 0∑l​(Hkl​−ESkl​)cl​=0. Hückel then makes three approximations:

  • Hkk=αH_{kk} = \alphaHkk​=α, the Coulomb integral, the same for every carbon;
  • Hkl=βH_{kl} = \betaHkl​=β, the resonance integral, when kkk and lll are bonded neighbours, and zero otherwise;
  • Skl=δklS_{kl} = \delta_{kl}Skl​=δkl​, so overlap between different atoms is neglected.

For a ring the Hamiltonian matrix becomes

H=α 1+β A,\mathbf{H} = \alpha\,\mathbf{1} + \beta\,\mathbf{A},H=α1+βA,

where A\mathbf{A}A is the adjacency matrix of the ring: Akl=1A_{kl} = 1Akl​=1 when l=k±1(mod6)l = k \pm 1 \pmod 6l=k±1(mod6) and zero otherwise. An eigenvector of A\mathbf{A}A with eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ is an MO with energy E=α+λβE = \alpha + \lambda\betaE=α+λβ, so the whole problem is to diagonalise a 6 × 6 matrix of zeros and ones. Written as a secular determinant, det⁡(A−λ1)=0\det(\mathbf{A} - \lambda\mathbf{1}) = 0det(A−λ1)=0 expands to

λ6−6λ4+9λ2−4=(λ−2)(λ+2)(λ−1)2(λ+1)2=0.\lambda^6 - 6\lambda^4 + 9\lambda^2 - 4 = (\lambda - 2)(\lambda + 2)(\lambda - 1)^2(\lambda + 1)^2 = 0 .λ6−6λ4+9λ2−4=(λ−2)(λ+2)(λ−1)2(λ+1)2=0.

#Solving the ring without expanding the determinant

Every carbon in benzene has the same surroundings, so each row of A\mathbf{A}A is the row above it shifted one place to the right. Matrices with this property are called circulant, and their eigenvectors are discrete Fourier waves. Try ck=e2πijk/6c_k = e^{2\pi i jk/6}ck​=e2πijk/6 for an integer jjj:

(Ac)k=ck+1+ck−1=(e2πij/6+e−2πij/6)ck=2cos⁡ ⁣(2πj6)ck.(\mathbf{A}c)_k = c_{k+1} + c_{k-1} = \left(e^{2\pi i j/6} + e^{-2\pi i j/6}\right) c_k = 2\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi j}{6}\right) c_k .(Ac)k​=ck+1​+ck−1​=(e2πij/6+e−2πij/6)ck​=2cos(62πj​)ck​.

The ring closes on itself, ck+6=ckc_{k+6} = c_kck+6​=ck​, which is why jjj must be an integer, and only six values give different vectors: j=0,±1,±2,3j = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, 3j=0,±1,±2,3. The energies are

Ej=α+2βcos⁡ ⁣(2πj6),E_j = \alpha + 2\beta\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi j}{6}\right),Ej​=α+2βcos(62πj​),

which gives α+2β\alpha + 2\betaα+2β for j=0j = 0j=0, α+β\alpha + \betaα+β for j=±1j = \pm 1j=±1, α−β\alpha - \betaα−β for j=±2j = \pm 2j=±2 and α−2β\alpha - 2\betaα−2β for j=3j = 3j=3.

#Why jjj and −j-j−j are degenerate

The cosine is even, so Ej=E−jE_j = E_{-j}Ej​=E−j​. Physically, e2πijk/6e^{2\pi i jk/6}e2πijk/6 is a wave running round the ring in one direction and e−2πijk/6e^{-2\pi i jk/6}e−2πijk/6 is the same wave running the other way. Neither direction is preferred, so the two have the same energy. The two vectors are complex conjugates of each other, and because A\mathbf{A}A is real, their sum and difference are also eigenvectors with the same eigenvalue. That gives real coefficients:

ck(j,cos⁡)∝cos⁡ ⁣(2πjk6),ck(j,sin⁡)∝sin⁡ ⁣(2πjk6).c_k^{(j,\cos)} \propto \cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi jk}{6}\right), \qquad c_k^{(j,\sin)} \propto \sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi jk}{6}\right).ck(j,cos)​∝cos(62πjk​),ck(j,sin)​∝sin(62πjk​).

They are normalised so that ∑kck2=1\sum_k c_k^2 = 1∑k​ck2​=1, which is the right normalisation once overlap is neglected. For j=1j = 1j=1 and j=2j = 2j=2 both sums of squares equal 3, so the factor is 1/31/\sqrt{3}1/3​. For j=0j = 0j=0 and j=3j = 3j=3 the sine is zero at every atom and only the cosine survives, with factor 1/61/\sqrt{6}1/6​. These two levels have no partner, because −0=0-0 = 0−0=0 and −3≡3(mod6)-3 \equiv 3 \pmod 6−3≡3(mod6), so they are single.

Any rotation of a degenerate pair, cos⁡γ ψcos⁡+sin⁡γ ψsin⁡\cos\gamma\,\psi_{\cos} + \sin\gamma\,\psi_{\sin}cosγψcos​+sinγψsin​, is an equally valid orbital. The figure uses the cosine and sine partners, a choice of orientation rather than a physical fact.

#Why α+2β\alpha + 2\betaα+2β is the lowest level

Both α\alphaα and β\betaβ are negative. α\alphaα is roughly the energy of an electron in a carbon 2p orbital inside the molecule. β=⟨ϕk∣H^∣ϕk+1⟩\beta = \langle \phi_k|\hat H|\phi_{k+1}\rangleβ=⟨ϕk​∣H^∣ϕk+1​⟩ couples two neighbouring p orbitals. Its integrand is largest between the two nuclei, where both orbitals have the same sign and the potential energy is strongly negative, so β<0\beta < 0β<0. With β<0\beta < 0β<0, the larger the coefficient of β\betaβ, the lower the energy. The nodeless j=0j = 0j=0 orbital at α+2β\alpha + 2\betaα+2β is therefore the most stable, and the fully alternating j=3j = 3j=3 orbital at α−2β\alpha - 2\betaα−2β is the least.

#Six π electrons and the 4n+2 rule

Benzene has six π electrons, one from each carbon. They fill the three bonding orbitals: two in α+2β\alpha + 2\betaα+2β and four in the degenerate α+β\alpha + \betaα+β pair. In the D6hD_{6h}D6h​ point group the levels are labelled a2ua_{2u}a2u​, e1ge_{1g}e1g​, e2ue_{2u}e2u​ and b2gb_{2g}b2g​ in order of increasing energy, so the HOMO is the degenerate e1ge_{1g}e1g​ pair and the LUMO is the degenerate e2ue_{2u}e2u​ pair. The total π energy is

Eπ=2(α+2β)+4(α+β)=6α+8β.E_\pi = 2(\alpha + 2\beta) + 4(\alpha + \beta) = 6\alpha + 8\beta .Eπ​=2(α+2β)+4(α+β)=6α+8β.

Three isolated ethylene π bonds would give 3×2(α+β)=6α+6β3 \times 2(\alpha + \beta) = 6\alpha + 6\beta3×2(α+β)=6α+6β. The difference, 2β2\beta2β, is the Hückel delocalisation energy.

The same pattern holds for any ring of NNN carbons, since the energies α+2βcos⁡(2πj/N)\alpha + 2\beta\cos(2\pi j/N)α+2βcos(2πj/N) always have one single level at the bottom and degenerate pairs above it (plus a single level at the top when NNN is even). A closed shell needs two electrons for the bottom level and four for each filled pair, so 4n+24n + 24n+2 π electrons with nnn filled pairs. This is the 4n+2 rule that now carries Hückel's name, and benzene is the case n=1n = 1n=1 [5].

#Checking the numbers

The snippet below builds the adjacency matrix, prints its characteristic polynomial, diagonalises it with numpy, and checks that the cosine and sine vectors above are eigenvectors.

import numpy as np

N = 6
A = np.zeros((N, N))                  # adjacency matrix of the ring
for k in range(N):
    A[k, (k + 1) % N] = A[(k + 1) % N, k] = 1.0

print("characteristic polynomial:", np.round(np.poly(A)).astype(int))

# H = alpha*1 + beta*A, so each eigenvalue lam of A gives E = alpha + lam*beta
lam, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(A)
for x in lam[::-1]:                   # beta < 0: largest lam is lowest E
    print(f"E = alpha {x:+.3f} beta")

# the cos/sin vectors from the text are eigenvectors of A
k = np.arange(N)
for j in range(4):
    for f in (np.cos, np.sin):
        c = f(2 * np.pi * j * k / N)
        if np.allclose(c, 0):
            continue                  # sin with j = 0 or 3 vanishes
        c = c / np.linalg.norm(c)
        x = c @ A @ c
        assert np.allclose(A @ c, x * c)
        print(f"j={j} {f.__name__}: lam={x:+.0f}  c={np.round(c, 3) + 0.0}")

#From coefficients to an orbital in space

The coefficients say how much of each carbon's p orbital goes into an MO. To draw the MO, each ϕk\phi_kϕk​ needs a shape. The figure uses a Slater-type orbital centred on carbon kkk:

ϕk(r)=N z e−ζ ∣r−Rk∣,N=ζ5/π,\phi_k(\mathbf r) = N\,z\,e^{-\zeta\,|\mathbf r - \mathbf R_k|}, \qquad N = \sqrt{\zeta^5/\pi},ϕk​(r)=Nze−ζ∣r−Rk​∣,N=ζ5/π​,

which is the n=2n = 2n=2, l=1l = 1l=1, m=0m = 0m=0 Slater function (the factor zzz is rcos⁡θr\cos\thetarcosθ measured from the nucleus, and every carbon has zk=0z_k = 0zk​=0).

The exponent comes from Slater's rules [2]. Carbon is (1s)2(2s,2p)4(1s)^2(2s,2p)^4(1s)2(2s,2p)4. For a 2p2p2p electron, each of the other three electrons in the (2s,2p)(2s,2p)(2s,2p) group screens 0.35 of a nuclear charge and each of the two 1s1s1s electrons screens 0.85:

S=3×0.35+2×0.85=1.05+1.70=2.75,S = 3 \times 0.35 + 2 \times 0.85 = 1.05 + 1.70 = 2.75,S=3×0.35+2×0.85=1.05+1.70=2.75,
Zeff=Z−S=6−2.75=3.25,ζ=Zeffn=3.252=1.625 a0−1.Z_\mathrm{eff} = Z - S = 6 - 2.75 = 3.25, \qquad \zeta = \frac{Z_\mathrm{eff}}{n} = \frac{3.25}{2} = 1.625\ a_0^{-1}.Zeff​=Z−S=6−2.75=3.25,ζ=nZeff​​=23.25​=1.625 a0−1​.

For n≤3n \le 3n≤3 Slater's effective principal quantum number equals nnn, so the divisor is 2. The geometry is in ångströms, so the exponent has to be converted: 1.625/0.529177=3.071.625 / 0.529177 = 3.071.625/0.529177=3.07 Å⁻¹. Along the zzz axis above a nucleus, z e−ζzz\,e^{-\zeta z}ze−ζz peaks at z=1/ζ=0.615 a0z = 1/\zeta = 0.615\,a_0z=1/ζ=0.615a0​, which is 0.33 Å. That is where each lobe is densest.

Two simplifications remain. First, neighbouring 2pz2p_z2pz​ orbitals do overlap. With this exponent and spacing the π overlap integral is S01=e−p(1+p+2p2/5+p3/15)=0.248S_{01} = e^{-p}(1 + p + 2p^2/5 + p^3/15) = 0.248S01​=e−p(1+p+2p2/5+p3/15)=0.248, where p=ζRCC=4.27p = \zeta R_\mathrm{CC} = 4.27p=ζRCC​=4.27 (the closed form of Mulliken and co-workers [3], confirmed by numerical integration). So Skl=δklS_{kl} = \delta_{kl}Skl​=δkl​ is a real approximation and the MOs are not exactly normalised in three dimensions. That does not matter for the figure, because the sampler only uses ratios of ∣ψ∣2|\psi|^2∣ψ∣2. Second, Hückel theory with a fixed Slater exponent gives the right ordering and nodal structure, not quantitative orbital energies; those need a self-consistent calculation such as Kohn–Sham DFT.

#Drawing the probability density with points

The figure places a few thousand points so that their density follows ∣ψ(r)∣2|\psi(\mathbf r)|^2∣ψ(r)∣2. It uses rejection sampling inside a box around the molecule:

  1. Find an upper bound pmaxp_\mathrm{max}pmax​ on ∣ψ∣2|\psi|^2∣ψ∣2 inside the box.
  2. Draw a point u\mathbf uu uniformly in the box.
  3. Keep it with probability ∣ψ(u)∣2/pmax|\psi(\mathbf u)|^2 / p_\mathrm{max}∣ψ(u)∣2/pmax​, that is, keep it if a second uniform number in [0,1)[0, 1)[0,1) is smaller than that ratio.
  4. Repeat until enough points have been kept.

The chance that a trial point lands in a small volume dVdVdV and is kept is proportional to ∣ψ∣2 dV|\psi|^2\,dV∣ψ∣2dV, so the kept points follow ∣ψ∣2|\psi|^2∣ψ∣2 exactly within the box. The bound must really be an upper bound: if ∣ψ∣2/pmax|\psi|^2/p_\mathrm{max}∣ψ∣2/pmax​ exceeds 1 anywhere, the densest regions are clipped and look flatter than they are. The box must also hold almost all of the density without wasting most trials.

A box of ±3.4 Å in xxx and yyy and ±2.2 Å in zzz holds more than 99.9 per cent of ∣ψ∣2|\psi|^2∣ψ∣2 for all six MOs. Estimating the maximum on a 61 × 61 × 61 grid comes close: the true maximum is at most 3.2 per cent above the grid value for these six MOs, so a 5 per cent margin is safe. A bound that is safe by construction exists, ∣ψ∣≤∑k∣ck∣/(ζe)|\psi| \le \sum_k |c_k| / (\zeta e)∣ψ∣≤∑k​∣ck​∣/(ζe), because no unnormalised z e−ζrz\,e^{-\zeta r}ze−ζr exceeds 1/(ζe)1/(\zeta e)1/(ζe), but it accepts 15 to 40 times fewer points. With the grid estimate, roughly 1 to 3 per cent of trials are kept, so 4,000 points cost a few hundred thousand evaluations of ψ\psiψ, each a sum of six exponentials.

import numpy as np

BOHR = 0.529177                       # angstrom
ZETA = 1.625 / BOHR                   # 3.07 per angstrom
ang = 2 * np.pi * np.arange(6) / 6
CARBONS = 1.39 * np.stack([np.cos(ang), np.sin(ang), np.zeros(6)], axis=1)

def psi(p, c):
    """Unnormalised pi MO at points p (shape (n, 3), angstrom)."""
    d = p[:, None, :] - CARBONS[None, :, :]          # (n, 6, 3)
    r = np.linalg.norm(d, axis=2)
    return (c * d[:, :, 2] * np.exp(-ZETA * r)).sum(axis=1)

def sample(c, n_points, rng, half=(3.4, 3.4, 2.2)):
    half = np.array(half)
    g = np.linspace(-1, 1, 61)[:, None] * half       # grid estimate of max |psi|^2
    grid = np.stack(np.meshgrid(*g.T, indexing="ij"), -1).reshape(-1, 3)
    p_max = 1.05 * (psi(grid, c) ** 2).max()          # 5 % safety margin
    kept, tried = [], 0
    while sum(len(k) for k in kept) < n_points:
        u = rng.uniform(-half, half, size=(50_000, 3))
        accept = rng.uniform(size=len(u)) < psi(u, c) ** 2 / p_max
        kept.append(u[accept])
        tried += len(u)
    pts = np.concatenate(kept)
    rate = len(pts) / tried                           # fraction accepted
    pts = pts[:n_points]
    return pts, np.sign(psi(pts, c)), rate

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
k = np.arange(6)
c = np.cos(2 * np.pi * k / 6) / np.sqrt(3)            # one e1g (HOMO) partner
pts, sign, rate = sample(c, 4000, rng)
print(f"acceptance {rate:.3f}; + points {np.mean(sign > 0):.3f}; "
      f"above plane {np.mean(pts[:, 2] > 0):.3f}")

acceptance 0.013; + points 0.499; above plane 0.497

Each point is then coloured by the sign of ψ\psiψ at that point: vermilion where ψ>0\psi > 0ψ>0 and teal where ψ<0\psi < 0ψ<0. The density ∣ψ∣2|\psi|^2∣ψ∣2 alone cannot show sign, and the relative sign of neighbouring lobes is what separates bonding from antibonding contacts. The overall sign of an MO carries no meaning, since ψ\psiψ and −ψ-\psi−ψ describe the same state, so swapping the two colours everywhere would show the same orbital.

#Why points and not an isosurface

Most orbital pictures are isosurfaces: the surface on which ∣ψ∣|\psi|∣ψ∣ equals a chosen value, with one colour for each sign. The size of that surface depends entirely on the chosen value, which is a display setting and not a property of the molecule. Raise it and the lobes shrink towards wherever ∣ψ∣|\psi|∣ψ∣ is largest; lower it and lobes of the same sign merge into larger shapes. A surface also has a hard edge, which suggests the electron is inside it and nowhere else.

A point cloud has no threshold. The expected number of points in any region is proportional to the probability of finding the electron there, so regions can be compared fairly: dense where the electron is likely, sparse in the tails, and nothing hidden behind a cut-off. The price is noise. A region expected to hold nnn points holds roughly n±nn \pm \sqrt{n}n±n​, so faint features need more points before they can be trusted, and in a flat projection points at different depths overlap.

#Nodal planes

Every π MO is built from 2pz2p_z2pz​ orbitals, and each of those is odd in zzz, so ψ(x,y,−z)=−ψ(x,y,z)\psi(x, y, -z) = -\psi(x, y, z)ψ(x,y,−z)=−ψ(x,y,z). The molecular plane is therefore a nodal plane of every π orbital, and in the figure each region above the plane has the opposite colour to its mirror image below it.

On top of that, the MOs have vertical nodes, which contain the sixfold axis. The coefficient pattern cos⁡(2πjk/6)\cos(2\pi jk/6)cos(2πjk/6) or sin⁡(2πjk/6)\sin(2\pi jk/6)sin(2πjk/6) changes sign 2∣j∣2|j|2∣j∣ times going round the ring, which gives ∣j∣|j|∣j∣ vertical nodal planes:

MO D6hD_{6h}D6h​ label Energy jjj Vertical nodal planes Electrons in benzene
1 a2ua_{2u}a2u​ α+2β\alpha + 2\betaα+2β 0 0 2
2, 3 e1ge_{1g}e1g​ (HOMO) α+β\alpha + \betaα+β ±1 1 4
4, 5 e2ue_{2u}e2u​ (LUMO) α−β\alpha - \betaα−β ±2 2 0
6 b2gb_{2g}b2g​ α−2β\alpha - 2\betaα−2β 3 3 0

The count rises with energy because each extra node turns more bonding contacts between neighbours into antibonding ones. In the j=3j = 3j=3 orbital every pair of neighbours has opposite signs.

The figure also shows a detail that textbook drawings smooth over. Most of these nodes are exact planes, because the orbital changes sign under a mirror reflection of the hexagon. That holds for both e1ge_{1g}e1g​ partners, for the sine partner of e2ue_{2u}e2u​ and for b2gb_{2g}b2g​. The cosine partner of e2ue_{2u}e2u​, with coefficients (1,−12,−12,1,−12,−12)/3(1, -\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, 1, -\tfrac12, -\tfrac12)/\sqrt3(1,−21​,−21​,1,−21​,−21​)/3​, has no such mirror. Its two nodal surfaces cross at right angles on the sixfold axis but bend as they move outwards: 0.33 Å above the plane, the node sits at about 44° from the direction of carbon 0 near the axis and at about 35° at the radius of the carbon ring. They still count as two nodal planes, because they divide the ring into the same four sign regions.

Finally, each partner of a degenerate pair has lower symmetry than the molecule, but the pair together does not: the summed density ∣ψcos⁡∣2+∣ψsin⁡∣2|\psi_{\cos}|^2 + |\psi_{\sin}|^2∣ψcos​∣2+∣ψsin​∣2 of the e1ge_{1g}e1g​ HOMO pair, which holds four of the six π electrons, is unchanged by a 60° rotation about the axis.
